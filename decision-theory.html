Foundations & The Decision Taxonomy

Decision theory is the formal analysis of rational behavior. Before applying mathematical axioms and probability calculus, we must strictly define the boundaries of inquiry: who decides, against what environment, and by what standard?

Three Perspectives The Decision Triad Instrumental Rationality

Normative vs. Descriptive vs. Prescriptive

Approach Guiding Question Discipline & Methodology Primary Goal
Normative (NDT) "How ought rational agents decide?" Philosophy, Mathematical Economics, Axiomatic Logic Establish formal consistency, coherence, and optimal value maximization.
Descriptive (DDT) "How do human beings actually decide?" Cognitive Psychology, Behavioral Economics Discover empirical heuristics, systematic biases, and cognitive limits.
Prescriptive "How can imperfect people be helped to decide better?" Management Science, Operations Research Design practical decision-support frameworks and debiasing nudges.

The Triad of Decision Sciences

In his opening lecture, Prof. Spieksma contrasts individual choice against strategic games and social choice:

  • Individual Decision Theory: A single agent chooses an act facing a passive environment ("Nature"). Nature has no hidden agenda, malice, or counter-strategy.
  • Game Theory: Two or more strategic agents interact. The optimal act of player A depends directly on player B's anticipation of A's choices (Nash equilibrium).
  • Social Choice Theory: Aggregating conflicting individual preferences into a single legitimate collective social outcome (Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem).

Rationality vs. Rightness: Avoiding Outcome Bias

Core Tenet · Instrumental Rationality

In decision theory, rationality is instrumental: an act is rational if and only if it represents the most effective means to attain the agent's chosen goals, given their current information and beliefs.

Classic Exam Trap · Outcome Bias (Slide Teaser)

Question from Lecture: "Suppose the PSV Eindhoven coach substitutes Ricardo Pepi for Guus Til in the 85th minute, and PSV concedes a tying goal. Was the decision irrational?"

Never conflate rational decisions with good outcomes! An act is evaluated as rational strictly based on the information, probability distributions, and expected values available at the moment the decision was made. A thoroughly rational decision can lead to an unfortunate outcome due to an unlucky state of Nature; conversely, an irrational gamble can occasionally get lucky.

Formalizing Decisions & Interactive Slide Showcase

To solve a decision problem mathematically, real-world complexity must be distilled into a formal mathematical triple: Acts, States of the World, and Outcomes.

The Formal Mathematical Triple: ⟨A, S, O⟩

A decision problem under risk or ignorance is completely formalized by the triple ⟨A, S, O⟩:

  • Acts (A = {a1, a2, ..., am}): Mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive actions available to the agent.
  • States (S = {s1, s2, ..., sn}): Mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive possible external conditions of the world.
  • Outcomes (O): The consequences resulting from taking act ai when state sj obtains:
    ai: S → O,   where   ai(sj) = oij
Crucial Axiom · Causal Independence Condition

In a standard decision matrix, the states must be causally independent of the acts chosen:

P(sj | ai) = P(sj)   for all acts ai and states sj

If an act directly alters the probability of a state (e.g., taking an umbrella somehow causes storm clouds to form), a single fixed probability distribution over columns cannot be specified without invoking Causal Decision Theory.

1. The Raincoat Dilemma (Risk & Expected Utility)

Morning in Eindhoven. Forecast predicts rain with probability p = 0.80, so no rain is 1 - p = 0.20.

Act \ State s1: Rains (p = 0.80) s2: No Rain (1 - p = 0.20)
a1: Wear Raincoat Damp but protected (+1) Sweaty, burdened (−1)
a2: Leave at Home Soaked to the bone (−2) Dry & free (+2)

Preference Order: Dry (+2) ≻ Damp (+1) ≻ Sweaty (−1) ≻ Soaked (−2).

EU(a1) = 0.80 × (1) + 0.20 × (−1) = 0.80 − 0.20 = +0.60
EU(a2) = 0.80 × (−2) + 0.20 × (2) = −1.60 + 0.40 = −1.20

Optimal Act under 80% forecast: Wear Raincoat (a1).

2. Leximin Tie-Breaking (Lecture Slide Example)

From Prof. Spieksma's slide: When acts tie on their worst-case outcome, Leximin breaks the tie by inspecting the 2nd-worst outcome.

Act State 1 State 2 State 3 State 4 Worst Outcome 2nd-Worst
Act 1 8 12 −1 2 −1 2
Act 2 0 7 −1 12 −1 0
Act 3 7 0 13 −1 −1 0
Act 4 16 −1 6 9 −1 6 (Winner!)

All 4 acts tie with worst outcome −1. Inspecting the 2nd-worst: Act 4 has 6, beating Act 1 (2) and Acts 2 & 3 (0). Leximin chooses Act 4.

3. Minimax Regret & Failure of IIA (Lecture Slide Example)

A famous slide demonstration: Minimax Regret violates Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (IIA).

Initial Problem S1 S2 S3 Max Regret
A1: (0, 10, 4) Regret: 5 − 0 = 5 Regret: 10 − 10 = 0 Regret: 10 − 4 = 6 6 (Winner!)
A2: (5, 2, 10) Regret: 5 − 5 = 0 Regret: 10 − 2 = 8 Regret: 10 − 10 = 0 8

Column Maximums: S1=5, S2=10, S3=10. Minimax Regret prefers A1 (6 < 8).

Now introduce an irrelevant alternative A3 = (10, 5, 1). The new column max for S1 becomes 10:

With A3 Added S1 (Max 10) S2 (Max 10) S3 (Max 10) New Max Regret
A1 10 − 0 = 10 10 − 10 = 0 10 − 4 = 6 10
A2 10 − 5 = 5 10 − 2 = 8 10 − 10 = 0 8 (Winner!)
A3 10 − 10 = 0 10 − 5 = 5 10 − 1 = 9 9

Adding the irrelevant option A3 reversed the preference between A1 and A2 (from A1 to A2). This proves Minimax Regret violates IIA!

4. Math in Sport: The Conference Dinner Matrix

Measurement Theory: Scales of Value & Invariance

Before performing arithmetic on utilities, we must understand what the numbers signify. Measurement theory dictates what mathematical transformations are permissible without destroying empirical meaning.

Stevens' Typology Affine Invariance Proof Effective vs Transformative

Stevens' Scales of Measurement

Scale Type Empirical Meaning Preserved Admissible Transformation Meaningful Operations Everyday Examples
Nominal Equivalence (= vs ≠) Any one-to-one relabeling Mode, Frequency counts Student ID numbers, jersey numbers
Ordinal Strict ranking order (x ≻ y) Any strictly increasing function: g(x) = φ(f(x)) Median, Percentiles, Rank correlation Mohs hardness, hotel stars, exam ranks
Interval Ratios of differences: (w − x)/(y − z) Positive affine: g(x) = a·f(x) + b   (a > 0) Mean, Variance, Expected Utility Celsius/Fahrenheit, vNM Utility u(o)
Ratio Direct ratios: x / y Positive scaling: g(x) = a·x   (a > 0) Geometric mean, Ratios ("twice as much") Length, Mass, Kelvin, Money (€)
Core Theorem · Why Ordinal Numbers Cannot Yield Expected Utility

Suppose an agent ordinally ranks four outcomes: Dry (4) ≻ Damp (3) ≻ Sweaty (2) ≻ Soaked (1).

Because any monotonic function is valid on an ordinal scale, we could apply a transformation turning the numbers into (1000, 4, 3, 1). The ranking of outcomes is identical, but calculating ∑ pi ui will completely reverse which act appears optimal! Hence, ordinal scales are strictly incompatible with Expected Utility calculations.

Mathematical Proof: Invariance of Expected Utility Under Affine Transformations

Formal Theorem & Step-by-Step Proof

Theorem: The preference ranking over acts induced by the Principle of Maximizing Expected Utility is invariant under any positive affine transformation of the utility function.

Step 1: Let act A have expected utility EU(A) = ∑ pi u(oi), and act B have EU(B) = ∑ qj u(oj').

Step 2: Apply positive affine transform u*(x) = a·u(x) + b with a > 0:
EU*(A) = ∑ pi [a·u(oi) + b] = a ∑ pi u(oi) + b ∑ pi = a·EU(A) + b   (since ∑ pi = 1).

Step 3: Compare transformed acts:
EU*(A) ≥ EU*(B) ⇔ a·EU(A) + b ≥ a·EU(B) + b ⇔ a·EU(A) ≥ a·EU(B).

Step 4: Since a > 0, dividing by a preserves the inequality:
EU(A) ≥ EU(B).   □

Takeaway: Shifting utility by constant b or multiplying by positive constant a never changes decisions under risk. Utility in NDT is an interval scale.

Effective vs. Transformative Decision Rules (Peterson Box 2.2 & Slide)

  • Effective Decision Rules: A rule that directly singles out a set of recommended acts from the given decision problem (e.g., MEU, Maximin, Maximax, Dominance).
  • Transformative Decision Rules: A rule that modifies the formal representation of the problem before solving it (e.g., merging repetitive states, or applying Laplace's Principle of Insufficient Reason to assign equal probabilities).

Decisions Under Ignorance: Slide Tables & Steps

When nature's probabilities are completely unknown, rational agents use non-probabilistic decision rules. Here are the five classic rules, explained with concrete slide tables and step-by-step procedures.

1. Dominance 2. Maximin 3. Leximin 4. Maximax 5. Hurwicz 6. Minimax Regret 7. Insufficient Reason

1. Principle of Dominance

Definition & Test

• Weak Dominance: Act A is at least as good as Act B in all states, and strictly better in at least one state.

• Strict Dominance: Act A is strictly better than Act B in every possible state.

Golden Rule: Never choose a dominated act!

Act \ State State 1 State 2 State 3 Verdict
Act 1 10 8 15 Dominates Act 2 (10>7, 8>5, 15>12)
Act 2 7 5 12 Strictly Dominated (Eliminate!)

2. Maximin (Wald's Pessimistic Rule)

How to Apply Maximin
  1. For each act, inspect the row and write down its worst outcome (row minimum).
  2. Choose the act whose worst outcome is the highest (the "best of the worst").
Act State 1 State 2 State 3 Worst Outcome Maximin Choice
Act 1 15 5 2 2 —
Act 2 10 8 6 6 —
Act 3 7 7 7 7 Selected by Maximin (7 > 6 > 2)

3. Leximin (Lexicographic Maximin)

From the Slide · Breaking Ties in Maximin

If two or more acts tie on their worst outcome, compare their second-worst outcomes. If they tie again, compare third-worst, and so on.

Slide Example (Source 8):

Act State 1 State 2 State 3 State 4 1st Worst 2nd Worst
Act 1 8 12 −1 2 −1 2
Act 2 0 7 −1 12 −1 0
Act 3 7 0 13 −1 −1 0
Act 4 16 −1 6 9 −1 6 (Winner!)

All acts tie at −1. Leximin breaks the tie with the second-worst: Act 4 has 6, beating Act 1 (2) and Acts 2 & 3 (0).

4. Maximax (The Optimist / Gambler Rule)

How to Apply Maximax
  1. For each act, inspect the row and write down its best outcome (row maximum).
  2. Choose the act with the highest maximum (the "best of the best").
Act State 1 State 2 State 3 Best Outcome Maximax Choice
Act 1 15 5 2 15 Selected by Maximax (15 > 10 > 7)
Act 2 10 8 6 10 —
Act 3 7 7 7 7 —

5. The Optimism-Pessimism Rule (Hurwicz α)

Simple Weighted Value

Balances upside and downside via an optimism parameter α between 0 and 1:

Hurwicz Value = α × (Best Outcome) + (1 − α) × (Worst Outcome)

• If α = 1: pure Maximax (total optimism).
• If α = 0: pure Maximin (total pessimism).

Act Row Minimum (Worst) Row Maximum (Best) Calculation for α = 0.7 Hurwicz Value
Act 1 10 100 0.7 × 100 + 0.3 × 10 = 70 + 3 73
Act 2 50 90 0.7 × 90 + 0.3 × 50 = 63 + 15 78 (Winner!)

Even though Act 1 has a higher peak (100 vs 90), Act 2's much safer floor (50 vs 10) gives it a higher Hurwicz score of 78.

6. Minimax Regret (Savage)

Three Step Procedure
  1. Find Column Maximums: For each state (column), identify the highest payoff achievable.
  2. Compute Regret Matrix: Regret = (Column Max) − (Cell Payoff).
  3. Minimax: Find each act's worst regret (row max). Choose the act with the lowest worst regret.
Act \ State s1 (Col Max: 15) s2 (Col Max: 8) s3 (Col Max: 7) Max Regret
Act 1 15 − 15 = 0 8 − 5 = 3 7 − 2 = 5 5 (Tied Winner!)
Act 2 15 − 10 = 5 8 − 8 = 0 7 − 6 = 1 5 (Tied Winner!)
Act 3 15 − 7 = 8 8 − 7 = 1 7 − 7 = 0 8

7. Principle of Insufficient Reason (Laplace)

Transformative Rule · Assume Equal Probabilities

If there is no reason to believe one state is more probable than another, assign each of the n states equal probability 1/n. Then choose the act with the highest simple arithmetic average.

Act State 1 (1/3) State 2 (1/3) State 3 (1/3) Average Score
Act 1 15 5 2 (15 + 5 + 2) / 3 = 7.33
Act 2 10 8 6 (10 + 8 + 6) / 3 = 8.00 (Winner!)
Act 3 7 7 7 (7 + 7 + 7) / 3 = 7.00

Exercise 1: Lindbergh's Atlantic Crossing Peterson Ex. 1.3

(a) Was Charles Lindbergh's decision to fly across the Atlantic Ocean in 1927 a decision under risk or a decision under ignorance?

(b) Is booking a commercial transatlantic flight today a decision under risk or under ignorance?

View Peterson's Official Solution

(a) Decision under ignorance: In 1927, long-distance transatlantic aviation was in its infancy. Lindbergh had virtually no reliable empirical track record, meteorological models, or statistical samples to assign meaningful numerical probabilities to engine failure, oceanic storms, or disorientation. Hence, he acted under ignorance.

(b) Decision under risk: Modern commercial aviation has accumulated millions of verified commercial flights over decades. Robust global data provides precise actuarial accident rates (roughly 1 fatal accident per several million flights). The passenger decides under risk.

Exercise 3: Ordinal vs. Cardinal & Interval vs. Ratio Peterson Ex. 2.7

Explain precisely the difference between:

(a) Ordinal scales and cardinal scales.

(b) Interval scales and ratio scales.

View Peterson's Official Solution

(a) Ordinal vs. Cardinal: An ordinal scale conveys only ranking information (x ≻ y), allowing arbitrary monotonic increasing transformations. A cardinal scale (which includes both interval and ratio scales) conveys quantitative magnitude, allowing differences between values to be meaningfully compared.

(b) Interval vs. Ratio: An interval scale has an arbitrary zero point (e.g., Celsius), preserving ratios of differences under positive affine transformations g(x) = a·x + b. A ratio scale possesses an absolute, non-arbitrary physical zero (e.g., Kelvin, mass), preserving direct ratios x/y under linear scaling g(x) = a·x.

Exercise 4: University League Tables & Scales Peterson Ex. 2.8

(a) What type of scale is used for published rankings of universities (e.g., 1st, 2nd, 3rd...)?

(b) Is an ordinal scale appropriate if universities are scored on composite weighted metrics (student-staff ratio, citations, research funding)?

View Peterson's Official Solution

(a) Ordinal scale: The final league table ranks institutions ordinally (rank 1, rank 2, rank 3...).

(b) Methodological tension: While the reported result is ordinal, the underlying methodology aggregates multiple ratio and interval metrics (citations, faculty salary, funding). Converting rich interval metrics into a raw ordinal rank discards critical information: the gap between Rank 1 and Rank 2 might be 0.1 points, while between Rank 2 and Rank 3 it might be 25 points!

Exercise 5: Ignorance Decision Matrix Analysis Peterson Ex. 3.1 & 3.2

Consider the following decision matrix under ignorance with utilities:

Act \ State s1 s2 s3
a1 15 5 2
a2 10 8 6
a3 7 7 7

(a) Which act is chosen by Maximin?

(b) Which act is chosen by Maximax?

(c) Construct the Regret matrix and determine the Minimax Regret act.

View Complete Step-by-Step Solution

(a) Maximin: Minimums are min(a1) = 2, min(a2) = 6, min(a3) = 7. The maximum minimum is 7 ⇒ Maximin selects a3.

(b) Maximax: Maximums are max(a1) = 15, max(a2) = 10, max(a3) = 7. The maximum maximum is 15 ⇒ Maximax selects a1.

(c) Minimax Regret:

Column maximums: s1 = 15, s2 = 8, s3 = 7. Regret = Column Max − Cell Payoff:

Act s1 s2 s3 Max Regret
a1 15 − 15 = 0 8 − 5 = 3 7 − 2 = 5 5
a2 15 − 10 = 5 8 − 8 = 0 7 − 6 = 1 5
a3 15 − 7 = 8 8 − 7 = 1 7 − 7 = 0 8

The minimum maximum regret is min(5, 5, 8) = 5, tying between a1 and a2.

Exercise 6: Leximin vs. Maximin Advantage Peterson Ex. 3.3

What is the fundamental theoretical and practical advantage of Leximin compared to standard Maximin?

View Peterson's Official Solution

Standard Maximin looks only at the single worst outcome of an act and ignores all other information. If multiple acts share the exact same worst-case outcome, Maximin is completely paralyzed and declares them equally good—even if one act offers fantastic payoffs in all other states while the other act offers near-worst payoffs everywhere else!

Leximin's advantage: It breaks ties lexicographically by systematically examining the 2nd-worst, 3rd-worst, etc. outcomes, ensuring that acts with superior non-worst performance are rationally prioritized.
